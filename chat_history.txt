The upper bound (the packing itself). In Friedman's survey, the reference for the 17-square packing is literally "J. Bidwell, 1998, private communication" — Bidwell, an undergraduate at the University of Hawaii, found it by improving Hämäläinen's 1980 packing (which used squares at 45°), and it's the smallest example where the best known packing uses squares at three different angles. It was never published as a paper; it was sent to Friedman, verified, and entered into the survey, which is how a large fraction of results in this field exist (Cottingham, Cantrell, Stenlund, Trump's n=11, etc. are all "private communication" citations too).

The number itself comes from a standard procedure once you have the configuration:

Fix the contact structure — the combinatorial data of which square touches which square/wall along which edge or corner. In Bidwell's packing, 10 squares sit axis-aligned around the border and 7 tilted squares are wedged in the middle, and the whole thing is rigid: every tilted square is pinned by its neighbors.
Parametrize the configuration by the unknowns: the tilt angles, the positions of the tilted squares, and the container side s.
Each tangency (corner-on-edge contact) gives one polynomial equation in sines/cosines of the angles and the coordinates. Rigidity means you get exactly as many independent equations as unknowns.
Solve the system; s falls out as an algebraic number.
Friedman's survey shows this recipe explicitly for simpler cases. For instance, in the 272-squares-in-side-17 packing, the whole thing reduces to one angle: the tilted squares sit at angle θ, and Ellsworth found in 2023 that the optimum is θ ≈ 28.5506°, with the side given in closed form as s = 4cos θ + sin θ + 13. And for his own 29-square packing, the survey writes down the system: the tilted squares contact the others in 3 places, and the contact lengths, the side s, and the tilt angle θ satisfy a system of equations solved numerically. The 17-square case is the same game, just with more unknowns because there are two nontrivial angle families instead of one, so there's no tidy closed form — the modern high-precision solve of Bidwell's contact system gives s(17) ≤ 4.67553009360455. That constant is a root of the polynomial system defined by the contact structure; nobody has exhibited a pleasant minimal polynomial for it.

The lower bound (the only actual proof content for n = 17). The proven side is much weaker: s(17) ≥ (40√2 + 19)/17 ≈ 4.4452, due to Trevor Green (2000, also unpublished private communication). The technique is the unavoidable-set method: to show s(n) ≥ k, you exhibit a set P of n−1 points in the side-k square such that every unit square placed inside must contain a point of P; then n squares can't fit in anything smaller, since a packing would need n distinct points but only n−1 exist. The engine behind these sets is a stack of geometric lemmas — e.g., any unit square in the corner region must contain the point (1,1), and any unit square whose center lies in a triangle with all sides ≤ 1 must contain one of the triangle's vertices. Green's set for 17 (Figure 34 in the survey) uses 16 cleverly placed points, and the specific value (40√2 + 19)/17 emerges from optimizing where those points can sit while keeping the covering property.

So the state of n = 17 specifically: 4.4452 ≤ s(17) ≤ 4.6756, a gap of about 0.23, and the trivial bound s(17) > 4 is barely improved. Nobody has proven Bidwell's packing optimal, and given that n = 11 — with far fewer degrees of freedom — remains unproven despite Trump's packing being rigid and 45 years old, 17 isn't close. The reason the picture looks so unsettling is exactly the derivation structure: the configuration is the solution of a rigid contact system, and there's no reason for the roots of such a system to look symmetric or pretty. In principle the interval-arithmetic branch-and-bound approach (the one being used in Vienna for small n) could someday certify it, but the search space over 17 squares × free rotations is currently out of reach.

One footnote since the meme version gets it wrong: the viral image is often captioned as "the optimal packing." It's the best known packing. If you found something below 4.6755, you'd have a publishable result — that's the actual bar.
